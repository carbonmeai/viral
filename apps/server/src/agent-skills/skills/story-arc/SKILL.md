# Story arc and virality

Every short is judged on two things: the **story arc** (how its beats are ordered) and the **virality
radar** (why someone keeps watching and shares it).

## Choosing the format from the kind of video

The kind of video decides the format. Judge it from the whole transcript and the contact sheets, then lead with the
format that suits it:

| What the video is | Format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Stuff happens, the talking is slow**: challenges, food, experiments, builds, DIY, science, travel, pranks, transformations | **narrated** (`claim-interleaved` when the people react along the way, `question-recap` when the best reaction comes at the end) | the action is great but the talking drags: a narrator tells the story and the real people answer in their own voice |
| **The people talking are the content**: podcasts, interviews, debates, talk shows, rants, tips, sports calls, gaming commentary, stand-up | **`cut-down`** | the speakers' own words carry it; a narrator would talk over the best part |
| **"Top N / best X" across many videos**: rescues, fails, saves, goals, transformations, reactions | **ranking** (several videos only) | each video is one entry; a single video never makes a ranking |
| **A topic, no single video**: a person "through the years", a news recap, history, "how X happened" | **narrated** over clips from several videos (topic mode, below) | the narrator joins clips that don't tell the story on their own |

When it's unclear, narrated suits "stuff happens" videos and cut-down suits "people talk" videos. If the talking is
weak but the visuals are strong, go narrated.

**The creator's best format is the narrated short that tells the whole story** (their "10 Levels of Spicy" short):
the narrator sets up each moment and the real person delivers the punchline in their own voice, stage after stage,
to the real payoff:

> Narrator: "This man says he's not scared of anything." → Rush: **"I am not scared of anything."**
> Narrator: "…Pepper X. 538 jalapeños in one bite." → Rush: **"And you want me to eat it?"**
> Narrator: "He signed a waiver, and ate it." → Rush: **"Oh gosh, my heart is beating out of my chest."**

## The intensity curve

Intensity goes up, dips, then climbs higher than before:

```
intensity
   ▲                                         ╱ PAYOFF
   │            CONFLICT                   ╱ RISING ACTION
   │          ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲                 ╱
   │   RISING╱           ╲    COMEBACK   ╱
   │  ACTION╱             ╲___________ _╱
   │  HOOK ╱
   └──────────────────────────────────────────▶ progression
```

| Beat | What it does | Budget in a 45–60s short |
|---|---|---|
| `hook` | A pattern interrupt in the first 1–3s: a shocking claim, the result, a question, or the most visual moment. It must open a loop. | 1.5–4s |
| `rising_action` | Context and stakes, as fast as possible. Why should I care, and what's at risk? | 5–12s |
| `conflict` | The problem, failure, twist or "it went wrong" moment. The first peak. | 4–10s |
| `comeback` | The dip and turn: the realisation, the fix, the "but then…". | 4–10s |
| `rising_action` | A second build toward the answer. Optional for short clips. | 3–10s |
| `payoff` | Closes the loop the hook opened. The best line, result or punchline. The story ends on it hard; only the closing question follows. | 3–8s |

When the source has no setback (a straight tip or reveal), use the simpler **hook → rising action →
climax → resolution**: map climax to `conflict` or `payoff`, and resolution to `payoff`.

**Beats come from anywhere in the long video.** A short doesn't have to be one continuous span. Take the
best hook line, the best context, the best twist and the best payoff from wherever they are, as long as
the result makes sense on its own.

## Mapping a long video

Write down its beats with timestamps: **goal → process → setback → fix → result → best real reaction**.
The setback becomes the conflict, the fix the comeback, and the result plus the reaction the payoff.

**Map the WHOLE story before choosing anything**: every stage (all ten levels, every attempt), the rules and stakes,
the setbacks, and the 6–10 best real lines (boasts, pain, confessions, twists, the final reaction). A narrated short
that covers the full story keeps viewers; a highlight reel that skips stages loses them.

## The virality radar: score every candidate 0–10

| Axis | Question | High score looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Open loop** | Does the hook create a question the viewer *needs* answered? | "I lost $10k in one day…", "This one rule changed everything", showing the result before the how |
| **Completion compulsion** | Does the structure make leaving feel like missing out? | A clear arc with a visible destination: countdowns, before/after, "wait for it", a payoff in the last seconds |
| **Universal relatability** | Would someone with no context get it and see themselves in it? | Money, failure, relationships, work, health, everyday frustrations. No insider jargon, no "as I said earlier" |
| **Shareability** | Would someone send it to a friend or comment? | A hot take, a surprising number, a funny moment, a tip worth saving |

A strong short is a **big triangle**: high on all three main axes. One spike (relatable but no open
loop, say) is a weak short, so fix the hook or drop the candidate.

## Cutting rules

- **Hook first, always.** If the strongest line is at minute 12, it goes at 0:00 even when the rest of the
  short comes from minute 10. Showing the result first is a valid teaser hook, but then the payoff must
  *add* something (the how or why), not repeat it.
- **No dead air at the start.** Start on the first word, not on a breath, "so", "um" or "hey guys".
- **Cut filler inside beats.** Split a long stretch to skip a tangent. Every boundary is a jump cut, which
  helps the pacing.
- **Self-contained.** Remove anything that needs the long video ("like I mentioned", "in part 2").
- **Never cut anyone mid-sentence.** Every clip that plays a person's own voice (a real line, an ending, a
  cut-down section) starts on the first word of a sentence and ends on the last word of one. Only a pause over
  1.5 s counts as a sentence end, because people pause mid-sentence. Never end on a half-answer: if a reply runs
  long, keep it whole and cut somewhere else.
- **The story ends on the payoff.** No "so yeah", no fade, no filler after it: the payoff (the real reaction, the
  result, the punchline) is the story's last beat. When a real payoff runs as several clips a few seconds apart,
  take it as one continuous stretch so no line is skipped.
- **Then the closing question.** After the payoff, a short ends by asking viewers to comment and subscribe (see
  "The closing question" below).
- **Length:** a cut-down short runs 30–120 s; a narrated recap about 45–60 s; a narrated short that tells a whole
  story 60–120 s; a ranking about 20–25 s an entry. Never over 3 minutes (the Shorts limit).
- **Hook text** restates the open loop in 7 words or fewer and makes sense with the sound off.
- **Never invent facts or words.** Everything comes from what the video shows or says.
- **No sponsor reads, ads, QR codes or end screens, ever.**

## Scripts from several videos

- **One narrator ties the videos together.** The first time a video's footage or voice comes in, a narrated
  line says who or what it is ("Then a chemist tried the same candy").
- **Every real line names its video** (`video`: its V number) and is quoted from that video's transcript,
  at that video's seconds. Never move a quote to a video that didn't say it.
- **Use the fewest videos that tell the story.** A video earns its place with a line or a moment only it has.

## Topic mode: a narrated short about a topic, not one video

A person "through the years", a news story, a piece of history: the videos were found for the topic, and none tells
the story alone.

- **Facts come from sources.** State only what the videos say (their transcripts) or what the creator's brief gives.
  Never fill a gap from memory; if a fact the story needs isn't there, leave it out.
- **Only narrate what you have footage for**, and let the subject's real quotes answer the narrator.
- **News clips draw copyright claims** (ET, E! News, AP, broadcasters). Prefer the subject's own footage and
  interviews; when a short leans on news footage, say so in your reasoning so the creator knows the risk.

## The closing question

Every short (narrated, ranking and cut-down) ends, after its payoff, with **one question for the comments plus a
subscribe ask**, on a held last frame with the question on screen. It's on by default; a style can turn it off.

- **The question is about the viewer, tied to this short's topic**, easy to answer in a word or a number: "What's
  the hottest pepper you've ever eaten?", "Which comeback got you?", "Would you have eaten it?"
- **The line:** the question, then "Tell us in the comments, and subscribe for more." About 10–16 words in all.
- **Never a question the short already answered**, never a generic "like and subscribe" without a question, and
  never before the payoff: the story ends first.
- It's written in its own place when a stage asks for it (a script's last beat, `Cta`, with just the question; a
  cut-down plan's `question`), never as one of the story's beats, narrated lines or real lines.
